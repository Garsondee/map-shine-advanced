## 1. The brief, in the author's own words

> The map is top down, birds eye view. `_Outdoors` allows us to smear that texture
> in a direction and create effectively a fake shadow of the building where the
> closer to the original dark pixels of `_Outdoors` the darker the shadow,
> essentially the shadows are always darker near the edge of the building. Then we
> have all the layers above the current floor which are blocking the ambient day
> light from reaching the ground. […] one way to fake this would be to make the
> upper floors black, offset them, then blur them. This would combine with the
> building shadow to create the darkest shadows where both the sky above and the
> walls around prevent light from reaching that point.

> As long as we have rational behaviour around map edges, a moving animated and
> evolving fake simulation of light and shadow generated by the sun then we'll win.
> It doesn't need to be physically accurate, it just needs to make sense.

> We need to include all overhead tiles, all background images and foreground
> images from all floors. We need it to be intelligent about picking contributors
> based on the floor that the user is on.

This is a **compositing** problem — silhouette, offset, blur, multiply — not a
ray-marching problem. Everything below follows from taking that literally.

## 2. THE LAW

> We cannot have a shadow which is generated by opaque occluders which ever goes
> from black to grey to white to grey to white. There should never be a case where
> a shadow has that curve when dealing with one light source and opaque occluders.

**Along the shadow direction, away from its caster, a shadow may only ever get
lighter.** Never darker, never lighter-then-darker. One light, opaque occluders,
monotonic. This is not a quality target to tune toward — it is a correctness
wall, and §5 makes the model satisfy it *by construction* rather than by tuning,
because two independent tuning-based attempts have now failed it.

Everything else (exact falloff curve, blur growth, strength) is look, and look is
the author's to dial.

## 3. Why the previous model has to go

Not "it needs more work" — it has two measured, structural defects and one
structural excess.

**Defect 1 — the combine could not compound.** Every term joined one
softmax-weighted mean. A mean can never leave the interval its inputs span, so a
weak second shadow *lightened* a strong first one. Measured on the real Tower
Bridge middle floor across 167 wall edges: building shadow alone `0.296`
visibility, sky-reach alone `0.769`, **both together `0.448` — brighter than the
building alone.** (Fixed 2026-08-02 by multiplying transmittances between
sources; the lesson is kept in `feedback_mean_cannot_compound_occluders` and the
regression guard survives into this model.)

**Defect 2 — the receiver read its own height from the *caster* channel.** Round
Six's *"you cannot be shadowed by something not taller than you"* compared the
receiver against a height that answered a different question. 41% of lit texels
immediately outside a wall are flat bridge deck and street carrying a 402 px
caster height, so a 300 px building's shadow switched off entirely on them:

| dist from wall | 0 | 30 | 75 | 140 | 230 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| receiver height 0 | **0.255** | 0.368 | 0.493 | 0.739 | 0.851 |
| receiver height 402 px | **0.654** | 0.677 | 0.638 | 0.697 | 0.588 |

The first row is a textbook shadow. The second is the author's reported bright
halo hugging the building, and its wandering curve is THE LAW being broken.
Author's ruling: **the model is wrong** — the receiver is the floor's ground, and
contributors are strictly things *above* it. §6 encodes exactly that.

**The excess.** Per-texel caster heights, a per-texel self-immunity rule, entry-
and crossing-distance bisection searches, lateral tap cones, a sharpened receiver
gate, height/coverage packed into separate channels that disagree at silhouette
edges. Every one of those was added to repair a symptom of the previous one. The
layer stack below needs none of them.

## 4. The model in one paragraph

Build a small **stack of occluder layers**. Each layer is a flat silhouette (a
coverage mask, 0..1) plus ONE height in world px — no per-texel heights, ever.
For each layer, smear its silhouette along the shadow direction by
`L = height / tan(elevation)`, weighted by a falloff that decays with distance so
the shadow is darkest against its caster. Multiply the layers' transmittances
together. That is the whole algorithm.

```
shadowDir  = the direction a shadow is thrown  (opposite the direction to the sun)
for each layer (silhouette S, height h, strength k):
    L          = clamp(h / tan(elevation))            // this layer's throw
    occ(p)     = max over d in [0, L] of  S(p − shadowDir·d) · falloff(d / L)
    T         *= 1 − occ(p) · k
visibility(p)  = T · edgeRamp(p)
```

**Why `max` inside a layer and `×` between layers.** Inside one layer the samples
are the same physical occluder seen from many distances — a union, so `max`;
compounding them is the p-norm bug that saturated an entire shadow to black.
Between layers they are genuinely different occluders in series — light that
clears the wall must still clear the deck overhead — so transmittances multiply.
That one distinction is the whole of `feedback_mean_cannot_compound_occluders`.

## 5. Why THE LAW cannot be broken here

Take a receiver `p`, move it further along the shadow direction by `δ`. Every
back-trace sample the new position can reach is one the old position could also
reach, but at a *greater* distance `d + δ`:

```
occ(p + shadowDir·δ) = max_d [ S(p + shadowDir·(δ − d)) · f(d) ]
                     = max_d′ [ S(p − shadowDir·d′) · f(d′ + δ) ]      (d′ = d − δ)
                     ≤ max_d′ [ S(p − shadowDir·d′) · f(d′) ]          (f is decreasing)
                     = occ(p)
```

**Occlusion can only decrease as you move away from the caster, for any silhouette
whatsoever, provided `f` is monotonically decreasing.** Not "for convex blobs",
not "for the cases we tested" — for any mask. The proof needs exactly one
property of `f`, which is a one-line assertion in the test suite.

And because a product of decreasing transmittances is itself decreasing, stacking
layers cannot break it either. THE LAW survives composition, which is precisely
what the previous model could not promise.

Two riders, both to be **measured, not assumed** (`feedback_measure_the_output_not_the_equation`):

- **Distance-growing blur.** Penumbra widening samples a coarser mip as `d` grows.
  Convolving a monotonic function with a symmetric kernel keeps it monotonic, but
  a *varying* kernel is not a plain convolution. Sweep it.
- **The discrete `max`.** A finite number of `d` samples can step between argmaxes.
  It cannot produce a *rise* (each sample is individually decreasing in `p`), but
  it can produce a visible stair. Sweep sample count for stair amplitude.

## 6. Contributors — which layers exist, for the floor the user is on

The receiver is **floor `k`'s ground**. A contributor is anything that stands above
that ground and blocks sky from reaching it. Membership, never a derived
threshold (`feedback_membership_beats_derived_threshold`).

For viewing floor `k`:

| # | Layer | Silhouette source | Height |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | This floor's **walls** | `_Outdoors` dark on floor `k` | wall height (the one authored slider) |
| 2 | This floor's **overhead** | overhead tiles hosted on floor `k` | the tile's own elevation above floor `k` |
| 3 | The floor **directly above** | floor `k+1` background ∪ foreground art alpha | `k+1`.bottomElevation − `k`.bottomElevation |
| 4 | **Everything higher**, merged | floors `k+2…` background ∪ foreground art alpha | nearest such floor's elevation above `k` |

Four layers, one RGBA texture, one channel each, four heights as a `vec4` uniform.
A hard, countable budget — this is what "dig our way out of the hole of
complexity" buys: the whole occluder description is **one texture and one vec4**.

Rules that fall out, and that the previous model got wrong:

- **A floor never occludes itself.** Floor `k`'s own background/foreground art is
  NOT a contributor. This is the direct fix for defect 2 — flat deck and street
  art stop being 402 px occluders, of themselves or anything else.
- **Floors below `k` never contribute.** They are under the receiver.
- **Walls come from `_Outdoors` only.** Art alpha describes where paint is, not
  where a wall stands. `_Outdoors` is the authored fact.
- **Foreground counts.** It is art above the floor's ground and it blocks sky.
  Explicitly named in the brief and previously ignored.
- **Merging layer 4 is a deliberate approximation.** Two floors far above throw
  nearly the same offset; merging them costs a few px of offset error and saves an
  unbounded layer count. Cheap and honest — and it is stated here so it is a
  decision rather than a silent cap (`no silent caps`).

The bridge case, as a sanity check the author named: standing on the lower floor
under the bridge, the deck above is layer 3 — a large silhouette at a small
offset. High sun ⇒ `L → 0` ⇒ the shadow sits directly under the deck. Low sun ⇒ it
slides out from under. Large shadow under the bridge, free, no special case.

## 7. Map edges

*"Rational behaviour around map edges."* Author's ruling, 2026-08-02:

> If the black pixels of `_Outdoors` extend across the edge of the map then we
> assume that this continues into the distance for the sake of edge of scene
> shadows.

So **clamp-to-edge is the correct rule, not a hazard to defend against** — and it
is also the cheapest, because it is what the sampler does natively. A building
whose footprint runs off the map keeps casting as though it continued; a border
texel that is open ground keeps reading as open ground. The silhouette's own
border content decides, which is exactly the authored fact
(`feedback_membership_beats_derived_threshold`).

1. **Clamp-to-edge on every layer sample.** Never wrap — wrap teleports a shadow
   to the opposite side of the map.
2. **No border scrubbing.** An earlier draft of this document proposed forcing the
   border clear before smearing to avoid black bands. That is now explicitly
   WRONG: it would truncate the shadow of every building that runs off the map,
   which is the case the author called out.
3. **The existing edge ramp is kept** for the outermost band, so the effect fades
   out rather than terminating on a hard line.

## 9. How it gets verified (Shader Lab, not eyeballs)

`bench-sun-shadow` already renders real derived Tower Bridge data through real
materials, and already has a whole-frame local-maximum detector. It gains:

1. **THE LAW check** — sweep outward from every real wall edge along the shadow
   direction; assert the profile never rises. Report the worst rise in world
   coordinates. This is the check the previous model would have failed on day one.
2. **A monotonic-`f` assertion** in the CPU twin — §5's single precondition.
3. **Compounding check, kept** — a second occluder never lightens (already written,
   already sabotage-tested).
4. **Layer isolation** — render each of the four layers alone and assert the
   combined result is at least as dark as the darkest single layer, everywhere.
5. **Bridge case** — assert the underside of the deck is dark at high sun and the
   shadow migrates outward as elevation falls.
6. **Edge behaviour** — assert no dark band is smeared inward from any border, and
   that off-map reads never produce a shadow with no caster.

Lab-green remains `BUILT (unverified)`. Only the author on a real scene says LIVE.

## 10. Open questions

- **Wall height source.** Layer 1's height is today one scene-wide slider
  (`buildingHeightPx`). Per-building heights would need an authored source that
  does not exist yet. Staying scene-wide for now; noted, not assumed.
- **Falloff shape.** `(1 − d/L)^e` is the incumbent. The exponent is look, and the
  author dials it. THE LAW only requires that it decrease.
- **Layer strengths.** Should an upper floor darken as hard as a wall? Probably
  softer — sky blocked from above still leaves horizon light. One `k` per layer,
  exposed, defaulting equal until the author says otherwise.
- **Ambient vs. sun.** This models the sun. The "sky blocked from above" term the
  author describes is arguably ambient occlusion and may want its own zero-offset
  pass. The layer stack supports it trivially — a layer with `L = 0` — so this is a
  look decision, not an architectural one.
