# Wind Simulation — Real-World Research (feeding Wind.md Tiers 2–3)

**Purpose:** Document-only research into how real wind behaves — magnitude scaling from calm to hurricane, building wakes, hour-to-hour diurnal change, and how to seed a noise-driven field for consistency — plus a paraphrased case study of a shipped AAA production wind system that solves nearly the same problem we do. **No design decisions are made in this document.** It exists so that whoever designs `Wind.md` Tier 2 (transient sim) and Tier 3 (emergent pressure) doesn't have to rediscover any of this from scratch. Tie-backs to our own architecture are called out explicitly and are all in §6, clearly marked as _things to consider_, not changes made.

**Source of record:** A multi-source web research pass (meteorology references, urban wind engineering standards, foundational graphics papers, GDC talks), adversarially cross-checked where noted, plus a full transcript of Sean Feely's (Sony Santa Monica Studio) GDC talk on _God of War_'s wind system, supplied by the author. Every claim below is cited; §9 has the full link list.

**Honesty note on how this was produced:** the automated research pass that gathered §1–4 hit the account's monthly spend limit partway through its own verification and synthesis steps — of 53 extracted claims, only 25 got a chance at 3-vote adversarial verification (11 confirmed, 1 refuted, 13 never adjudicated due to the cap, not because they were doubted), and the tool's own final "merge into one report" step never ran at all. Everything below was reconstructed by hand from the raw per-agent output on disk, not the tool's polished output. Confidence is still high — most claims trace to a primary/government/peer-reviewed source, and one (§2) was caught as a fabrication by a rigorous direct-source read — but flag this as a manually-assembled synthesis, not a machine-verified one.

## 1. Magnitude scaling — calm to hurricane in one control

**The Beaufort scale** is the standard observational classification: 13 bands (Force 0–12), each with a knot range and a physically observable land/sea effect. Confirmed directly against NOAA's Storm Prediction Center table, then independently cross-checked during adversarial verification against the US National Weather Service and the UK Met Office for at least one band (Force 6) — all three agree. Full range: Force 0 _Calm_ (&lt;1 kt, "smoke rises vertically") → Force 4 _Moderate breeze_ (11–16 kt, dust/leaves/loose paper lift) → Force 6 _Strong breeze_ (22–27 kt, large branches move, wires whistle) → Force 8 _Gale_ (34–40 kt, twigs snap, hard to walk into) → Force 12 _Hurricane-force_ (≥64 kt, "devastation"). An extended range (Force 13–17) exists for tropical-cyclone use in a few countries, but the WMO's 2012 standard only defines 0–12. Worth knowing: the scale is fundamentally qualitative — numeric knot equivalents were retrofitted after the category descriptions already existed (land-based descriptors were added by George Simpson in 1923), and the WMO itself calls the speed conversions non-binding. (NOAA SPC; Wikipedia/Beaufort scale)

There's also a closed-form curve relating band number to speed: **v ≈ 0.836 × B^1.5 m/s** (≈ 1.625 × B^1.5 knots) — wind speed grows super-linearly, not linearly, with each step up the scale. (Wikipedia/Beaufort scale)

For a genuinely _continuous_ statistical model rather than a 13-row lookup table, the **Weibull distribution** (two-parameter: scale _c_, shape _k_) is described as the standard, most-used model for wind-speed distributions in wind engineering — a real continuous family that spans the whole range from calm to extreme in one shape. (USDA ARS / WEPS erosion-modeling documentation, a real US government engineering tool — see §3)

**Two real shipped precedents for "one continuous control, not hand-authored states":**

- _Ghost of Tsushima_ (Sucker Punch): wind **direction** stays globally constant; **magnitude** varies continuously across space and time using Perlin noise — not discrete hand-authored wind states. (Game Developer / GDC 2021 write-up)
- _God of War_ (Sony Santa Monica): explicitly cites the Beaufort scale as inspiration, but for artist-facing tooling reduces its 12 bands down to a handful of **named** presets rather than exposing either raw physical units or a fully free-form continuous slider. Full detail in §5.5 — the lesson is complementary to Ghost of Tsushima's, not contradictory: the underlying driver can (and should) be continuous, but humans still need a small named vocabulary to calibrate against.

A useful mechanical note for noise-driven magnitude specifically: Bridson's curl-noise paper (see §2) observes that a single spatial length-scale parameter _L_, used to sample the underlying noise, simultaneously sets both the visual eddy/vortex size (~_L_) and an upper bound on the resulting flow speed (~_O_(1/_L_)) — literally one knob that scales a noise-driven field from "large, lazy swirls" to "small, fast turbulence." (Bridson, Houriham & Nordenstam, SIGGRAPH 2007)

## 2. Building wakes / "wind shadows"

The theoretical concern that motivated this research area holds up: **potential (irrotational) flow — which is what a Laplace-equation wall bake produces, i.e. exactly what Wind.md Tier 1's `bakeWindStructure` computes — cannot generate flow separation or a lee-side wake by construction.** That's a purely viscous, no-slip-boundary-layer phenomenon. Nothing found in this research contradicts that; if anything, it was reinforced by catching a fabricated counter-claim (below).

**A claim was checked and found to be a hallucination — worth recording because of how it was caught.** A secondary summary suggested that Jos Stam's classic "Real-Time Fluid Dynamics for Games" (the GDC paper Tier 1's own wall-bake is conceptually descended from) reproduces a genuine Von Kármán vortex street behind a sphere in its own validation results — i.e., that a simple advect+project+occupancy-grid solver gets a real wake "for free." An adversarial verification pass read all ~80 slides of the actual source PDF directly (not a secondary AI summary) and found this false: the vortex-street image is a scanned photograph reproduced from a textbook (Van Dyke's _An Album of Fluid Motion_), explicitly credited to outside photographers (Zabusky 1969, Thorpe 1971), shown on a "literature/inspiration" slide next to similarly-sourced Kelvin-Helmholtz and Rayleigh-Bénard photos — not the paper's own simulation output. Stam's actual obstacle handling (per the same slides) is a crude "copy the value from a neighboring cell" occupancy scheme, and the deck poses "Visual Accuracy?" as an open, unresolved question rather than claiming validation anywhere. Two lower-quality secondary sources (a search snippet and an AI-summarized mirror site) had apparently propagated a fabricated description. **Net: don't expect a wake to fall out of a simple grid-aware advection solver — the literature agrees it has to be added by hand.** (Stam, "Real-Time Fluid Dynamics for Games," GDC 2003 — verified directly against the primary PDF)

**The actual hand-add technique**, from the foundational SIGGRAPH 2007 curl-noise paper: sum in a **separate turbulence-noise term**, scaled to the obstacle's size, switched on via a **smooth amplitude/ramp function only in the region behind the object**, then add that to the underlying smooth laminar-flow potential (which is itself ramped to zero at solid boundaries). That's a concrete, directly portable recipe: keep the existing potential-flow bake for the "doesn't cross walls" structure, and layer a small hand-gated noise patch downwind of solid cells on top for "looks turbulent in the lee." The same paper also notes that octave-summed noise with a power-law amplitude falloff (matching the Kolmogorov turbulence energy spectrum) gives multi-octave noise a physically-grounded turbulent character rather than being an arbitrary visual trick. (Bridson, Houriham & Nordenstam, SIGGRAPH 2007)

**A second real, named technique**, also from Stam's paper: **vorticity confinement** (citing Fedkiw et al.) — a forcing term that re-injects small-scale rotational energy a numerically-dissipative solver loses, rather than needing a finer simulation grid. Worth knowing if a future transient sim (Tier 2) ever feels too smoothed-out.

**Obstacle representation rule** (Stam): a boolean occupancy grid works for blocking flow, but only reliably — without diagonal/thin-wall leaking — if the obstacle is **at least two grid cells thick**. This directly validates the "over-seal rather than leak" conservative rasterization already built into Tier 1's `rasterizeWallsToGrid`.

**Real-world pedestrian-comfort magnitude reference:** the **Lawson wind comfort criteria** (a genuine, named standard used in the AEC/architecture industry) gives concrete activity-based thresholds at pedestrian height (~1.5–1.75 m): 1.8 / 3.6 / 5.3 / 7.6 m/s for frequent-sitting / occasional-sitting / standing / fast-walking tolerance, with an "Uncomfortable" tier above 7.6 m/s (at &gt;2% annual exceedance probability) and a stricter "Unsafe" tier above 15 m/s (at &gt;0.022% exceedance). The source is explicit that this supplies the _magnitude/comfort_ side only — it does not itself provide wake-geometry data. (SimScale, citing the Lawson criteria)

**Gap, stated plainly:** no source produced an actual numeric "wake extends N building-heights downwind" ratio. The specific standard that would have it — the AIJ (Architectural Institute of Japan) guidelines for CFD around buildings (Yoshie et al., _J. Wind Eng. Ind. Aerodyn._) — was correctly identified as the right reference by the search phase, but the fetch hit a ScienceDirect paywall and returned no extractable content. If a specific wake-length rule of thumb matters later, that paper (or a wind-tunnel-data secondary source citing it) is the next thing to chase.

## 3. Diurnal variation — why wind changes hour to hour

**Core mechanism:** differential heating and cooling of the air layer near the ground creates a horizontal temperature difference, which creates a horizontal pressure difference, which drives wind from the cooler/higher-pressure side toward the warmer/lower-pressure side. This one mechanism underlies both the mountain/valley case below and the general flatland day/night cycle. (Zardi & Whiteman, "Diurnal Mountain Wind Systems," 2013)

**Mountain/valley systems** show this as a clean, recurring **four-phase daily cycle** — night phase → morning transition → day phase → evening transition — paced by the daily formation and breakup of a ground-level temperature inversion. As a rule: upslope/up-valley flow by day, downslope/down-valley flow by night. Concrete numbers: downslope (night) flow ~1–4 m/s, upslope (day) ~1–5 m/s, valley-center winds commonly 3–10 m/s (with valley-exit jets locally reaching ~13 m/s at ~200 m up, observed at the mouth of Austria's Inn Valley). Timing: the morning reversal begins shortly after sunrise once incoming solar radiation exceeds outgoing longwave loss, and in a Colorado case study the inversion took ~3–5 hours to fully break up; the evening reversal is slower to establish — sometimes not until several hours after sunset, up to roughly midnight. These circulations are **fragile**: a background/synoptic wind of only ~2–3 m/s can substantially disrupt or eliminate the local thermal circulation entirely. (Zardi & Whiteman, 2013)

**The general (flatland) version** is a boundary-layer regime switch: daytime surface heating creates a convective, well-mixed, turbulent boundary layer; at night the surface cools and the boundary layer becomes stable and "decouples" from the faster air above. Turbulence intensity swings measurably with this — roughly **~10% during the day (convective) vs. &lt;5% at night (stable)**. Wind direction can also visibly _veer_ (rotate with height) at night — up to ~15° across a typical near-surface layer — versus staying close to uniform by day. The **nocturnal low-level jet** (a fast ribbon of air a few hundred meters up, often moving faster than the air above or below it) is physically produced by Coriolis-induced wind veer combined with inertial oscillations (an Ekman-spiral-type mechanism), not by thermal forcing directly. (Wind Energy Science, Copernicus, 2022)

**The most directly implementable simplified model found:** a real, government-vetted engineering tool — the USDA Agricultural Research Service's WEPS wind-erosion model — drives its whole diurnal wind curve from just **three historical-statistics inputs**: the day's mean speed, the max/min speed ratio, and the historical hour-of-day of peak wind, combined into a smooth **cosine curve**. The documentation explicitly warns that drawing a fresh independent random sample every timestep — instead of reading a value off that smooth curve — produces an "uncorrelated" sequence that destroys realistic temporal coherence; which approach is right depends on whether the downstream use needs that coherence. A real worked example: Lubbock, TX had an annual diurnal cycle with max 6.55 m/s / min 4.19 m/s (ratio ≈ 1.56), peaking at hour 15 (3 pm). Wind _direction_ is handled separately in the same model, via inverse-CDF (Monte Carlo inversion) sampling against a historical 16-direction probability table. (USDA ARS, "Stochastic Wind Simulation for Erosion Modeling")

**GABLS3** is the standard reference benchmark shape modelers check simplified boundary-layer schemes against: a real, fully-instrumented 24-hour cycle (a 200 m tower at the KNMI-Cabauw site, six measurement heights, a specific real day in July 2006) that culminates in a nocturnal low-level jet, deliberately selected for clean textbook conditions (clear skies, strong nighttime radiative cooling &gt;30 W/m², no fog, steady geostrophic wind 5–19 m/s). Its own literature notes that timing/phase errors dominate the error budget more than fine numeric precision does — reassuring for a "reads as right" rather than "is physically exact" goal. (WEMEP docs, GABLS3 benchmark)

## 4. Seeding for consistency

**Core enabling idea** (Squirrel Eiserloh, GDC 2017, "Math for Game Programmers: Noise-Based RNG" — found at the primary source, as this research specifically set out to do): replace stateful, sequential RNGs — even good ones like Mersenne Twister — with **hash-based noise functions**. Their key property is **"unordered access"**: any output can be generated directly from an (index / position / seed) tuple with no sequential state to carry around. That's exactly the property that makes "the same seed always reproduces the same-feeling wind history, and any specific moment is independently reproducible" possible. Side benefits named in the talk: better reseeding, record/playback, network-loss tolerance, and lock-free parallelization — the last one is directly relevant to a GPU-resident field.

**A concrete decorrelation trick**, from Bridson's curl-noise paper (§2): a multi-channel noise field doesn't need a separately-implemented noise function per channel — sampling the **same** noise function at large, fixed spatial offsets per channel is sufficient to make the channels read as uncorrelated. This is worth flagging because it's exactly the pattern already in `world/wind-field.js#sampleWind()` — the arbitrary `float(11)` / `float(23)` / `float(41)` / `float(53)` offsets separating the dx/dy/flutter channels. Good to know that's a recognized technique from the literature, not an ad hoc hack.

**A coherence caution**, echoing §3: don't re-randomize every timestep — derive values from a smooth, seeded, continuously-evaluated function instead. This reinforces that the existing design (noise as a function of world position plus the one shared clock, never a per-frame re-roll) is the right shape, not a simplification to eventually "fix."

The single most directly actionable material on this topic, though, comes from the God of War case study — two production-proven noise-engineering fixes for problems this project hasn't hit yet but plausibly will. See §5.4.

## 5. Case study: _God of War_'s production wind system

**Source:** Sean Feely (Senior Staff Environment Tech Artist, Sony Santa Monica Studio), _"Interactive Wind and Vegetation in 'God of War'"_ — a GDC talk covering the wind system built for _God of War_ (2018). Primary hosting is GDC Vault (`https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1026036/Interactive-Wind-and-Vegetation-in`), which is access-gated and could not be fetched. The notes below are paraphrased from a full auto-generated YouTube transcript the author supplied (`https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MKX45_riWQA`) — treat exact wording as approximate (auto-captions occasionally mis-hear a word; one likely instance is flagged in §5.5), but the mechanisms themselves as reliable, since they're described in enough technical detail to cross-check internally for consistency.

### 5.1 System shape — an ambient/near-field split, discovered the hard way

Their wind lives in two parts that map closely onto Wind.md's own `A` (ambient) + `D` (baked/sim) split. A **wind volume**: a 3D texture centered near the player (pushed ~25% ahead of camera rather than fully centered, since less simulation detail is needed behind the player), footprint **32×32 m, 16 m tall, at 1 m³ voxel resolution** — giving an on-screen interactive footprint of roughly 24×24×12 m. Wind vectors are stored per-voxel (visualized as RGB = XYZ) and advanced each frame with a simple advection-diffusion simulation: render wind vectors in, blur, sample backwards to advect forward. Outside that volume, they originally tried using very large wind-motor primitives (their equivalent of a "contributor," §5.2) to drive whole-level wind — shipped in an E3 2016 build — and found it produced an **uneven gradient**: strong in the direction wind was blowing toward, weak looking back toward where it came from, because nothing outside the box was feeding momentum in from the edges. Their fix was to stop trying to make the volumetric sim cover everything, and instead use a simple **ambient wind vector** (direction + speed, plus an extra layer of noise for localized gust/calm pockets) for the far-field baseline, reserving the 3D sim volume purely for near-field, player-relevant dynamic effects.

### 5.2 Wind motors — their "contributor" model

Their **wind motor** is the direct analog of our `WindContributor`. Three base shapes (sphere / cylinder / cone — a cone is just a cylinder with one radius set to zero), each animatable (strength, position, orientation, scale), and four output distributions: **directional** (emits along its primary axis), **omni** (emits outward from a point), **vortex** (emits around its primary axis), and **wake** (a special fourth type: emits wind in the direction of travel, proportional to speed). The wake motor is by far their most-used primitive, largely because it rides for free on existing weapon-swing and attack animations. Its output isn't purely "backward along the travel direction" — they blend the pure direction-of-travel vector with an outward-from-center vector at equal weight before scaling by speed, producing a "pine cone" fan-out shape that reads better than a straight trail; and they smooth the motion input itself with a two-frame position average, which gives a bit of extra outward flare on tight curved paths (an axe swing). Amusingly, that two-frame averaging was originally an unintentional bug (computing distance between the current frame's position and the _previous_ frame's _previous_ position) that they kept because it looked better once already authored around.

### 5.3 Wind receivers — their "consumer" model

Four receiver types, in the order the talk covers them: **audio** (samples wind speed into a custom mix parameter — described as the least involved integration); **cloth** (each cloth object samples wind once at its transform center, applied to every simulation point scaled by the dot product of force direction and local cloth normal — explicitly **did not ship well**; overhauling the whole cloth system wasn't in scope for that project, and adding wind exposed pre-existing problems in it); **particles** (each particle samples local wind speed/direction and computes the impulse needed to match it, scaled by a per-particle "wind influence" value — conceptually identical to drag toward a moving-fluid target rather than a fixed one); and **meshes**, the deepest-developed receiver, covered separately below.

**Mesh wind** is authored with a small, deliberately curated parameter set. Two pieces of **per-vertex data**: a _wind mask_ (0–1, normalized distance from anchored "root" vertices that don't move — computed by a tool from mesh topology, not hand-painted per vertex) and the _root vertex's relative position_, stored as a delta rather than an absolute position (so translating the object doesn't require recomputing it, and the numbers stay small for precision). **Object-level parameters**, applied in the vertex shader: _movement scale_ (max travel distance for the freest vertices, scaled by the wind mask); _density_ (controls the spatial scale of the noise features — artists can set separate low-speed and high-speed values, blended as wind speed rises up to roughly 20–30 m/s); _bend_ (a gamma-curve exponent applied to the wind mask — 1.0 gives a straight lean, 2.0 gives a parabolic bend concentrated at the tips); _stretchiness_ (a partial length-preserving correction after vertices are translated rather than rotated, to avoid slow trig in the shader while still resisting unnatural stretching — their default is a **20% correction**, since some visible stretch usually reads better than a rigid full correction); and _stiffness_ (scales UV coordinates toward the root, similar in effect to density but keeping the effect centered there). On top of the raw wind sample, vertices actually lean toward a **spring-damped "sway" proxy** of the wind vector, not the raw sampled value directly — the wind pushes the spring, and the spring pulls back to center, smoothing a noisy per-frame signal into believable inertial motion, with separate spring-strength and damping parameters controlling how "springy" versus how quickly-settling a model feels. One elegant free trick: because the spring's momentum term is mathematically the derivative of its position, and the derivative of a sinusoidal motion is the same curve phase-shifted by a quarter cycle, they reuse the _already-computed_ momentum value as a stand-in position for a second, delayed layer of motion (used for tree leaves lagging behind branch sway) — no extra simulation state required, only while wind speed is decreasing (since that's when the outer structure is the one leading the motion).

### 5.4 Two noise-engineering traps, both directly relevant here

**Trap 1 — animating a noise "scale" makes the pattern visibly slide.** Naively scaling the input coordinate fed into a noise function (to change its apparent density/frequency) works fine if the scale is fixed — but if the scale is ever _animated_ (theirs blends between two density settings as wind speed changes), every feature in the noise visibly slides or scrolls as the scale changes, worse the farther a point is from the scale's center. **Their fix: logarithmic (power-of-2) octave binning.** Instead of continuously rescaling the noise input, they keep several noise samples fixed at power-of-2 scales (four octaves) and, when a continuous scale value is requested, use a triangle-shaped falloff centered on that value to blend the _contribution weights_ of the two nearest fixed octaves — the scales themselves never move, so nothing visibly slides, only fades between two fixed, stationary patterns. A bonus: neighboring objects with similar-but-not-identical scale values still share some noise features, since they're drawing from the same small set of fixed octaves. **Relevance:** our own `sampleWind()` currently scales `centerXY` directly by a fixed `WIND_SPACE_FREQ` constant, which is safe _because_ that constant is never animated today. Wind.md §8.2 lists `gustScalePx` as a live-tunable parameter (200–2000 px); if that value is ever blended or animated at runtime rather than only set once per scene, this exact sliding artifact is the predictable result, and this binning technique is the documented fix.

**Trap 2 — independently-advected per-object noise coordinates decorrelate over time and never recover.** If each wind-receiving object tracks its own texture-space UV offset into a shared noise field (advected each frame by the _local_ wind that object experiences — a "flow map," the same technique used for scrolling water or sky textures), then two objects that are neighbors in world space but experience even slightly different local wind directions or speeds will gradually drift apart in texture space, lose their shared noise features, and — critically — **never recover** even once the wind returns to uniform, because the accumulated distortion doesn't reset itself. Their fix evolved in stages: (1) periodically reset ("flow-flip") the tracked offset — but a hard reset causes a visible pop; (2) mask the pop with the classic two-buffer flow-map technique — track two offsets a half-cycle out of phase, cross-fade between their samples, and only reset whichever one is currently fully faded out — but resetting to a _fixed_ starting coordinate every time produces an unmistakable visible **loop**; (3) fix the loop by resetting to a **randomized** destination each flip (valid because their base noise is spectrally uniform everywhere — any coordinate is as good as any other) — but compute that random jump **once per flip event and share it across every object**, so neighbors still land on the same new shared coordinates and stay coherent with each other; (4) fix the remaining problem that objects moving at different wind speeds need to flip at different _rates_ (fast wind needs frequent resets to bound distortion; slow wind needs rare resets, or the cross-fade itself becomes the dominant visible motion) by triggering a flip on **accumulated texture-space distortion** rather than a fixed timer — and then, to keep objects at similar-but-not-identical speeds flipping in sync with each other, apply the **same power-of-2 logarithmic binning idea from Trap 1** to the flip _rate_: objects are grouped into speed bins that are exact powers of two apart, so neighboring bins flip at exactly double/half each other's rate and land in phase on alternating flips; an object that changes bins gets its fade rate nudged to resync with its new group by the next flip, or — if it's too far out of phase to catch up smoothly — is simply told to wait for the group's _next_ flip rather than snapping abruptly. **Relevance:** our current architecture samples a single shared noise field directly by _world position_ rather than tracking a per-object advected UV offset, which likely sidesteps this entire failure mode by construction. This becomes directly relevant only if a future Tier 2/3 feature gives individual elements (smoke puffs, dust, embers) their own independently-advected texture coordinates instead of resampling the shared field fresh every frame — worth re-checking against this specific trap if and when that happens, rather than assuming it can't occur here.

### 5.5 Beaufort-inspired artist-facing speed naming

The talk states they reduced Beaufort's 12 categories down to **five** agreeable named speed presets for their tools, used to standardize how artists and level designers talk about and test wind intensity. The auto-transcript clearly names four in sequence — **Calm, Breezy, Strong, Violent** — preceded by a garbled line ("our speed categories are still…") that, given the stated count of five, most plausibly represents the auto-captioner mis-hearing a fifth tier name, **"Still,"** as part of the surrounding sentence rather than a distinct word. Flagged as a plausible reconstruction from an imperfect machine transcript, not a confirmed fact. Named buttons in their internal debug tooling snap a wind-speed-override slider straight to each preset's value, so every artist and reviewer tests under the same handful of calibration points. The talk is explicit that the underlying numbers are **texture-scroll speed, not real wind speed** — "this is not PBR wind" — and that once this shared naming language existed, questions like "what wind speed should I author for" stopped coming up.

### 5.6 Design philosophy and lessons learned

Their explicit framing: **"believable, not realistic"** — a deliberate choice given the game's fantasy setting, not an accuracy target. Three stated goals for the system: the player should be able to observe that their own actions affected wind (appropriate, since the player character is a god); the player should be able to observe wind direction; and the player should be able to observe _spatial variety_ of wind speed/direction. A stated subtlety: "able to observe" and "must observe" are different goals — they explicitly aimed for restraint over spectacle, and note that if a system can't clear these three goals under most conditions, its added complexity isn't worth shipping.

Two honest lessons-learned, both cautionary: **cloth** integration is flagged as not having worked out — added late in a project that had already overhauled much of the cloth system, it surfaced pre-existing problems rather than solving new ones, and was deprioritized rather than fixed. **Particles** looked genuinely exciting in early isolated tests, but once tuned to share settings with the shared level/mesh wind (for scene-wide consistency), the result was described as _worse_ than the earlier isolated tests — strong ambient wind speeds could push particles off-screen before they even faded in, and the effects team frequently chose to simply not respond to wind at all when an effect looked better without it. The talk's own conclusion: _"if the effect looks better without responding to wind, well, you know, I've got to agree."_ This is a useful real-world data point for a "one shared field, every consumer samples it" architecture specifically: sometimes a consumer legitimately looks better ignoring the field, and that has to be a supported, non-hacky choice rather than something the architecture fights.

_(Two adjacent systems from the same talk — imposter/billboard-cloud vegetation LOD, and a top-down height-field system for grass trampling under moving characters — are not wind simulation and are omitted here as out of scope; the trampling system in particular could be worth its own look later as a "decaying spatial contributor" pattern, but that's a different feature.)_

## 6. Cross-reference — what this confirms vs. what's worth considering later

Nothing in this section is a decision. It's a map from what was found back to `Wind.md`, split into "this independently confirms a choice already made" and "this names a technique worth having on hand when Tier 2/3 design actually starts."

**Confirms existing Wind.md decisions:**

- **Resolution** (Wind.md §2, "~1 texel per grid square") — _God of War_ ships its actual interactive wind volume at literally **1 m³ voxels**. A shipped AAA title uses the same granularity already chosen here, for the same reason (architecture-scale features, not pixel-scale).
- **The `A` (ambient) + `D` (baked/sim) split** (Wind.md §1) — _God of War_ learned this exact lesson the hard way: giant wind-motor primitives for level-wide wind (their E3 2016 approach) produced an uneven gradient because nothing fed momentum in from outside the simulated volume, and the fix was to add a simple ambient vector for the far field and keep the volumetric sim for the near field only. Same shape, same reason, independently discovered.
- **Named presets over raw physical units** (Wind.md §8.3, "Still air / Draughty castle / Blustery day / Storm outside") — both _Ghost of Tsushima_'s pure-noise magnitude approach and _God of War_'s explicit Beaufort-inspired five-tier naming (§5.5) support a small named vocabulary layered over a continuous driver, rather than either raw units or fully discrete hand-authored states.
- **"For looks, not physics"** (Wind.md §10) — _God of War_'s stated philosophy ("this was never PBR for wind," numbers are texture-scroll-speed, not real wind speed) is the same ethos, arrived at independently by a shipped AAA team.
- **Conservative wall rasterization** (Wind.md §4, already built in Tier 1) — matches Stam's rule that solid obstacles need to be at least two grid cells thick to reliably block flow without leaking.
- **Per-effect `windResponse` gain** (Wind.md §8.1) — already provides the exact escape hatch _God of War_ discovered they needed once particles tuned to match shared wind looked worse than particles tuned in isolation. Worth knowing this isn't speculative over-engineering; it's a documented real need from a comparable production.

**Worth considering when Tier 2/3 design actually starts (not decided here):**

- **Logarithmic octave-binning** (§5.4, Trap 1) if `gustScalePx` is ever animated/blended live at runtime rather than only set once per scene — the current direct-scale approach in `sampleWind()` is safe only because that constant is presently fixed.
- **Flow-flip + shared-random-jump + power-of-2 speed-binning** (§5.4, Trap 2) as a pre-solved fix if a future feature ever gives individual smoke/dust/ember puffs their own independently-advected texture coordinates rather than resampling the shared field by world position each frame.
- **Hand-gated turbulence-behind-obstacles** (§2, Bridson) as the concrete mechanism for an eventual "building wake" feature — layered _on top of_, not replacing, the existing potential-flow bake.
- **A Beaufort-formula-flavored or Weibull-flavored continuous curve** for a future single "how strong is the wind right now" control, rather than inventing a curve shape from scratch.
- **The 3-statistic cosine diurnal model** (§3, USDA ARS) as a lightweight option if a "time of day" wind driver is ever wanted, well short of real atmospheric simulation.
- **Hash-based (Eiserloh-style) per-scene seeding** (§4) whenever the field needs a formal "same scene, same feel across sessions, this exact moment reproducible" concept — the existing per-channel offset constants in `sampleWind()` already follow the right _shape_ (§4), a real seed would extend that from a global constant to something per-scene.
- **The motor shape/behavior taxonomy** (sphere/cylinder/cone × directional/omni/vortex/wake, §5.2) as a vocabulary check if `WindContributor`'s `shape`/`kind` enums (Wind.md §5) ever need extending — today's enums (`point`/`disc`/`segment`/`region` × `thermal`/`vent`/`impulse`/`wake`/`ambient`) already cover comparable ground.
